---
title: Obinutuzumab beta in NMOSD — results and mechanism
date: 2026-09-08T19:05:40+09:00
---

Obinutuzumab beta (MIL62) strengthens an established treatment strategy in AQP4-positive NMOSD: **CD20-directed B-cell depletion**.

### What did the trial show?

The BeInmost phase 3 trial randomized 91 adults at 32 Chinese centers to obinutuzumab beta or placebo.

| Outcome | Obinutuzumab beta | Placebo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Participants | 45 | 46 |
| Adjudicated relapses | 2 (4.4%) | 21 (45.7%) |
| Annualized relapse rate | 0.092 | 1.180 |
| Grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events | 6.7% | 6.5% |

The hazard ratio for first relapse was **0.069 (95% CI 0.016–0.296)**, corresponding to a 93.1% reduction in relapse hazard.

Participants received 1,000 mg on days 1 and 15, with repeat dosing at weeks 25 and 27 if relapse-free. Both groups received an eight-week prednisone bridge, including tapering.

**Caveats:** Median follow-up at the primary analysis was approximately 25 weeks despite the 52-week endpoint. EDSS deterioration was not significantly reduced. One treated participant died following infection and multiorgan failure; investigators judged the death unrelated to treatment. Long-term safety remains uncertain. This placebo-controlled study does **not** establish superiority over rituximab or other biologics.[1]

### What makes MIL62 different?

MIL62 shares obinutuzumab’s amino acid sequence but has nearly complete **Fc afucosylation**. Removing core fucose strengthens binding to FcγRIIIa/CD16 on NK cells, enhancing antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Experimental comparisons showed stronger FcγRIIIa binding and ADCC than conventional obinutuzumab; clinical superiority cannot be inferred from these assays.[2]

Like obinutuzumab, MIL62 is a **type II anti-CD20 antibody**. Compared with type I antibodies such as rituximab, this class favors direct B-cell death and relatively less complement-dependent cytotoxicity.[3]

### Why can anti-CD20 work when antibody-producing cells lack CD20?

AQP4-IgG-producing plasmablasts can be CD20-negative, so anti-CD20 does not directly eliminate every antibody-producing cell.[4]

The mechanistic rationale is to **reduce the upstream B-cell pool that replenishes antibody-secreting cells**. Clonal links between peripheral B-cell compartments and AQP4-specific CSF plasmablasts support this interpretation.[5] It remains a mechanistic explanation rather than a pathway proven by the trial.

In the small phase Ib study, peripheral B cells rapidly fell below 5 cells/μL in most participants, accompanied by memory B-cell depletion and declining AQP4-IgG levels.[6] Peripheral depletion, however, does not prove eradication of tissue-resident autoreactive cells.

### My take

The key question is whether stronger depletion improves outcomes in patients inadequately controlled by rituximab—and whether that benefit outweighs long-term immune suppression. This trial does not yet answer that comparison.
