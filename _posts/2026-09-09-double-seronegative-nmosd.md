---
title: Double-seronegative NMOSD — a clinical syndrome in search of its biology
date: 2026-09-09
---

**Double-seronegative NMOSD is a meaningful clinical diagnosis, but its name does not establish a single underlying disease.** The distinction matters when interpreting prognosis, treatment response, and future biomarker studies.

### What the new paper found

Malvaso and colleagues' 2026 systematic review included **41 studies and 671 patients** negative for both AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG.[1]

| Finding | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Median age | 38.6 years |
| Female-to-male ratio | 1.5:1 |
| Reported relapsing course | 73.6% |
| Baseline annualized relapse rate | 1.08 in double-negative NMOSD, 0.84 in AQP4-positive NMOSD, 0.61 in MOGAD |
| Between-group ARR comparison | Not statistically significant: p = 0.08 |
| Maintenance treatment, pooled before/after rate ratio | 0.19; 95% CI 0.07–0.49 |
| Treatment-effect heterogeneity | I² = 90% |

The MOGAD comparator represented NMOSD-like presentations, limiting generalization to all MOGAD. Most patients underwent live cell-based antibody testing; longitudinal retesting was insufficiently documented.[1]

**Interpretation:** relapse prevention deserves attention, but these observational before/after data cannot establish an 81% causal treatment benefit. Regression to the mean, treatment selection, and differing follow-up can affect the estimate. A high relapse proportion in selected published cohorts is not an individual first-attack prognosis. Nor does a nonsignificant group comparison establish equal disease activity.[1]

### What “double seronegative” actually requires

Negative AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG results alone are insufficient to diagnose NMOSD. Under the 2015 international consensus criteria, AQP4-negative disease requires at least two different core clinical characteristics, dissemination in space, additional MRI requirements, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses. At least one presentation must be optic neuritis, acute myelitis with a longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesion, or area postrema syndrome with its required MRI correlate.[2]

These features can occur within one attack; two separate attacks are not universally required. An isolated antibody-negative optic neuritis or myelitis therefore should not automatically acquire this label.

The criteria intentionally demand greater clinical and radiological specificity when the defining antibody is absent. They also acknowledge possible heterogeneity. Meeting a syndrome definition establishes a reproducible clinical classification; it does not, by itself, identify the injured cell, antigen, or immune pathway.[2]

### Could these simply be false-negative antibody tests?

Some cases may be, but a negative result needs assay-specific interpretation.

In a blinded AQP4 assay-comparison study, cell-based assays had approximately **90–94% sensitivity**, outperforming ELISA and tissue immunofluorescence in that cohort.[3] These estimates are population-dependent and do not mean that a fixed percentage of today's double-negative patients secretly have AQP4 disease. They demonstrate why the testing method matters and why even a good assay is not infallible.

The same study left unresolved whether persistently negative NMOSD-like presentations reflected missed antibodies, phenocopies, or different mechanisms.[3] International criteria recommend considering repeat AQP4 testing in relapsing seronegative patients and before antibody- or B-cell-directed treatment.[2] Reassessment should be clinically driven; repeated negative results strengthen confidence in seronegativity without proving a new mechanism.

### Finally: is double-seronegative NMOSD really a different disease?

**My evidence-based interpretation is that it is currently best treated as a clinical category with potentially heterogeneous biology.**

Three claims should be kept separate:

| Claim | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Patients can have a convincing NMOSD syndrome without detectable AQP4-IgG or MOG-IgG | Supported by clinical cohorts and diagnostic frameworks.[1–3] |
| Every such patient has missed AQP4 disease or MOGAD | Not established; antibody negativity cannot identify the missing mechanism.[3] |
| All double-negative patients share one distinct disease mechanism | Also not established by the evidence reviewed here. |

To establish a distinct disease, I would look for a reproducible positive biological signature: shared pathology, a validated biomarker, or a coherent mechanism replicated in independent, carefully phenotyped cohorts. Similar symptoms and apparent responses to broad immunosuppression would not be enough, because neither uniquely identifies a disease.

The practical conclusion is to take the clinical syndrome seriously while keeping its biological classification open. **“Double negative” describes what testing has not detected; it does not yet tell us what unifies the patients.**
