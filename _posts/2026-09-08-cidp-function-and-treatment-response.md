---
title: CIDP beyond relapse prevention — function, disease activity and treatment response
date: 2026-09-08
---

In CIDP, treatment response can mean several different things: preventing deterioration, recovering strength, improving daily function, or reducing ongoing nerve injury. These outcomes are related, but they are not interchangeable.

An August 2026 EMJ poster review brings together ADHERE/ADHERE+ analyses addressing these questions. The most useful message is that **clinical improvement, low disability, and biological treatment effects need to be interpreted separately**.[1]

### What kind of evidence is this?

The source is a **conference poster review supported by argenx**, with disclosed industry relationships and medical writing assistance. It summarizes post-hoc clinical analyses and exploratory biomarker work; it is not a new randomized trial.

The findings are useful for generating questions about treatment goals and response heterogeneity. They should not receive the same evidential weight as prespecified randomized outcomes.

### 1. Start with the ADHERE design

| Phase | What happened | What it can tell us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Run-in, up to 12 weeks | Previous treatment was withdrawn in previously treated participants and worsening assessed | Helps establish active, treatment-dependent disease |
| Stage A, up to 12 weeks | Open-label subcutaneous efgartigimod | Identifies participants showing clinical improvement |
| Stage B, up to 48 weeks | Responders randomized to continued efgartigimod or placebo | Tests whether continued treatment prevents relapse among responders |
| ADHERE+ | Open-label extension | Describes longer-term trajectories, without a continuing randomized comparator |

In the original ADHERE trial, 322 participants entered Stage A and 221 were randomized in Stage B. Relapse occurred in 31/111 participants receiving efgartigimod and 59/110 receiving placebo: **27.9% versus 53.6%, HR 0.39 (95% CI 0.25–0.61)**.[2]

This is a randomized-withdrawal design enriched for response. The central question is therefore: **among patients who improve with efgartigimod, does continuing it help maintain that benefit?** It is not a direct comparison of efgartigimod with IVIg in an unselected CIDP population.

### 2. Recently diagnosed, treatment-naïve patients

Only **24 of the 322 Stage A participants** were treatment-naïve and diagnosed within the preceding year. Mean time since diagnosis was 2.4 months.

In this small open-label subgroup:

| Outcome | Reported finding |
| --- | --- |
| Confirmed evidence of clinical improvement | 21/24, or 87.5% |
| Median time to initial confirmed improvement | 39.5 days |
| INCAT change | −1.0 points |
| I-RODS centile metric change | +10.7 points |
| Dominant-hand grip strength | +15.3 kPa |
| Non-dominant-hand grip strength | +14.8 kPa |

The results suggest that treatment-naïve patients can improve across several domains. However, **87.5% is not a comparative efficacy estimate against IVIg or corticosteroids**. With 24 patients, no randomized comparator, and a post-hoc analysis, the finding remains exploratory.

The approximately six-week response time also argues against treating a single early visit as a definitive assessment of benefit.

### 3. Grip strength: early change and sustained gains

The review reports median times to first improvement of:

- **4.1 weeks:** grip strength increase of at least 8 kPa.
- **6.4 weeks:** I-RODS centile metric increase of at least 4 points.
- **8.3 weeks:** adjusted INCAT improvement of at least 1 point.

These thresholds were used in the reported analyses. Grip strength can capture an impairment-level change before a broader disability scale changes, but this does not mean grip strength is universally the best measure for every CIDP phenotype.

During Stage B, efgartigimod reduced the hazard of grip-strength deterioration by **71.5%**, using a decline of at least **16 kPa in either hand**: HR 0.2850, 95% CI 0.1517–0.5352. This is a post-hoc grip-strength endpoint, not the original primary relapse endpoint.

By ADHERE+ week 36, the reported proportions achieving gains of at least 8, 16, 24, and 32 kPa in either hand were **69.8%, 50.3%, 38.9%, and 24.8%**, respectively, relative to run-in baseline.

#### Why the baseline matters

Figure 2 uses **Stage A baseline**, after the withdrawal period, rather than the earlier **run-in baseline**. In participants with paired data, dominant-hand grip strength had fallen by approximately 8.2 kPa during run-in.

Consequently, some early improvement represents recovery from withdrawal-related worsening. The analysis relative to run-in baseline helps address whether gains extend beyond that recovery.

The extension remains encouraging, but changing denominators and the absence of a continuing placebo group limit causal conclusions about long-term incremental improvement.

### 4. INCAT 0–1: a useful goal, with an important denominator

The review reports that **77/196 Stage A responders, or 39.3%, reached INCAT 0 or 1 at some point during ADHERE+**. Their mean Stage A baseline INCAT score was 4.6.

Among those 77 patients:

- **62/77, or 80.5%,** maintained INCAT 0–1 for at least approximately three months.
- **54/77, or 70.1%,** maintained it for at least approximately six months.

[![Original Figure 3: Proportions of Stage A responders reaching INCAT 0 or 1]({{ site.url }}/assets/cidp-adhere-2026/figure-3.webp)]({{ site.url }}/assets/cidp-adhere-2026/figure-3.webp)

*Figure 3 from the EMJ review. The final bar represents each participant’s best assessment during ADHERE+, not a common fixed-time endpoint. Click to enlarge.*

These numbers should not become “40% of all CIDP patients achieve near-normal function.” The denominator is a selected responder population, and the endpoint is the **best value attained at any time**.

Likewise, the 70.1% persistence figure applies to the **77 who first attained the threshold**, not to all 196 participants.

INCAT 0–1 indicates little disability on the activities covered by INCAT. It does not establish absence of fatigue, pain, sensory symptoms, electrophysiological abnormalities, or ongoing treatment dependence.

My interpretation is that low disability is a reasonable outcome to pursue, but **low disability on treatment is not synonymous with remission off treatment**.

### 5. IgG signatures: target engagement is not response prediction

The exploratory glycoarray examined 16 individual glycolipid/phospholipid targets and 120 heteromeric complexes.

The findings were interesting but insufficient for a clinical antibody test:

| Observation | What needs caution |
| --- | --- |
| At least one raised IgG signal in 62% of CIDP samples versus 55% of healthy controls | A positive signal alone has limited disease specificity |
| Previously immunoglobulin-treated patients had the broadest repertoire | Prior treatment complicates interpretation |
| N-acetylgalactosamine-GD1a IgG positivity: 33.5% in responders versus 23.5% in non-responders | A numerical association is not a validated predictive biomarker |
| 19 of 20 commonly detected reactivities declined after Stage A | Declines occurred regardless of clinical response |

Efgartigimod blocks FcRn-mediated IgG recycling. A broad fall in measured IgG reactivity is therefore consistent with its pharmacology.

However, **lowering an antibody signal does not establish that the antibody was pathogenic, or that lowering it caused clinical improvement**. The reduction in non-responders makes that distinction particularly important.

These results support further investigation of CIDP heterogeneity. They do not yet justify selecting treatment using this glycoarray.

### 6. NfL: a contextual injury marker, not a treatment-selection test

The NfL analysis included **214 baseline samples**.

- Mean serum NfL was **18.9 pg/mL**, with substantial variability.
- **24.3%** had levels above 20 pg/mL.
- Age-adjusted NfL z-scores were higher in unstable active disease than in stable active disease.
- Baseline z-scores did **not** distinguish efgartigimod responders from non-responders.

Among responders with elevated baseline NfL, levels fell during Stage A and continued to decline in Stage B. However, the later decline occurred whether patients continued efgartigimod or received placebo, and later samples were limited.

This weakens the case for interpreting NfL as a treatment-specific response marker. The reported 20 pg/mL threshold should also not be treated as a universal diagnostic cutoff; the review emphasizes age dependence and lack of disease specificity.

The more defensible role is **additional context about axonal injury alongside clinical assessment**. A low value does not, by itself, establish inactive CIDP, and a high value does not identify which treatment will work.

### 7. Can efgartigimod improve what IVIg has not?

This is the clinical question I find most interesting.

The functional gains suggest that some patients retain recoverable function. But these analyses do **not** show that efgartigimod produces greater recovery than optimized IVIg in partial responders:

1. Stage B compared continuation with withdrawal after efgartigimod response.
2. Early gains can include recovery from worsening during withdrawal of previous therapy.
3. Extension data have no sustained randomized active comparator.
4. Functional improvement does not directly establish axonal regeneration.

Persistent disability could reflect ongoing immune-mediated dysfunction, accumulated structural damage, or both. Distinguishing these explanations matters before interpreting a plateau as a need for stronger immune treatment. This is a clinical interpretation, not a conclusion directly tested by these posters.

### What I would take forward

The review supports measuring **more than relapse alone**: strength, disability, and daily activity can change at different speeds.

It also reinforces a separation between three questions:

- **Is function improving?** Follow repeated clinical measures.
- **Is the biological target being affected?** IgG reduction can demonstrate pharmacological activity.
- **Is nerve injury continuing?** NfL may add context, but remains incompletely validated.

The most promising finding is sustained functional improvement in a subset of patients. The most important unresolved issue is how to identify that subset prospectively—especially among patients with residual disability despite existing treatment.
